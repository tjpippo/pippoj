Food Loops

From tracking food waste to preventing it, by redesigning restaurant surplus around the work kitchens already do.

My role
Research synthesis, concept development, interaction design, wireframing, visual design, usability testing
Team
Four designers: Palm, Batuhan, Ming and me
Context
UW HCDE, 2026. Restaurant food waste in Seattle
Methods
Interviews, competitive analysis, concept sketching, user flows, wireframes, usability testing, Figma prototyping

In short

Problem
Restaurants throw away good food, and staff have no time to stop and list it for donation.
What we learned
The barrier was labor, not willingness. Every extra step costs paid staff time.
What changed
We moved the product upstream: scan ingredients when deliveries arrive, so surplus is predicted before it happens.
Result
A Figma prototype tested with five users, a restaurant worker and a manager.

    The obvious version of the problem

    Restaurants throw away food that is still good. Food banks need food. Connect the two. That was the brief we gave ourselves, and the first idea followed automatically: a platform where a restaurant lists surplus, an organization claims it, and someone arranges a pickup.

    It is a reasonable idea. It is also the idea everybody has, which should have been the first clue.

    Talking to the people who would have to use it

    Rather than designing for food banks, we interviewed the people whose behavior the whole system depended on: restaurant owners, managers and floor staff. We asked how food actually moves through a kitchen. Three constraints came up again and again.

    Time
    Staff are already receiving stock, prepping, serving, cleaning and closing. A donation form filled in every time something might become surplus is not a small ask. It is another closing task.
    Labor cost
    Wanting to reduce waste is not the constraint. Every extra action costs paid staff time, and a restaurant's margin has no room for goodwill that takes twenty minutes a night.
    Coordination
    Food cannot simply be posted. Someone has to establish what is available, how much, whether it is safe, when it can be collected, who wants it, and whether the handoff happened.

    A donation problemturned out to bea workflow problem

    We had assumed restaurants needed an easier way to give food away. What they needed first was an easier way to know what was about to become surplus. The first framing is reactive: food goes bad, someone notices, someone lists it. The second is preventive, and it happens days earlier.

    Everything after this point follows from that.

    Three directions, and why we didn't pick just one

    We explored three concepts rather than committing early. Each solved a real part of the problem, and each failed somewhere specific.

    Concept exploration

    Pick a concept

    Concepts one and two shared a fatal dependency: both needed a member of staff to notice surplus and choose to record it. The scanner was different, because it attached itself to something that already happens. Someone handles every box that comes through the back door anyway.

    So we stopped treating them as alternatives. The scanner became the entry point, and the other two became what happens downstream of it. Design around an existing behavior rather than demanding a new one is the principle I took from this project and have used on everything since.

    One path, eight steps

    Once the scanner led, the whole product could be described as one continuous route, from a delivery arriving to food leaving in someone else's hands. Every screen we designed later serves one of these eight moments.

      The screens we finished at the end of the quarter do not cover this whole path. Scanning, the inventory list and listing creation stayed as wireframes, so the prototype shows the surrounding system of discovery, alerts and coordination rather than the capture moment the concept turns on.

      Status had to be readable at a glance

      A cook walking past a screen has about two seconds. So expiry stopped being a date to read and became a state to notice: safe, use soon, at risk. Color does the work a sentence would otherwise have to do, and the only item that offers an action is the one that needs it.

        Leave the day running without listing anything and the food simply expires. That is what happens today, and nobody sees it, because waste leaves in a trash bag. Food redistributed is a number a manager can put in front of an owner, which is why the home screen leads with it.

        The interface decision was mostly deletion

        The first inventory form asked for ingredient, category, weight, quantity, unit, purchase date, expiry, supplier and storage. Nine fields, at the exact moment a delivery is blocking a doorway.

        Scanning replaced eight of them. And because the system already knew the item, quantity and expiry, creating a surplus listing later needed almost no input: the listing screen reuses what the scan captured. The best interaction design in this project was removing fields, not adding features.

        Testing, in widening circles

        The first round was five participants aged 18 to 22. That round answered a narrow question, whether someone can navigate this, and we were careful not to let it answer a broader one. A student understanding a screen is not evidence that the screen survives a dinner service.

        So we widened it: a restaurant worker, then a manager. Each round asked a different question, and the manager's changed the product's framing from a tool into an operational report.

        Five general users: can they navigate it?
        Interaction and labeling fixed
        A restaurant worker: does it fit the shift?
        Scanner-first entry, fields removed
        A manager: does it make operational sense?
        Status hierarchy, and a tally worth reporting

        We also studied Waste No Food, an existing redistribution platform. The useful question was not which features it had but where it starts: after surplus already exists, and dependent on the donor to describe it. That gap is the whole argument for Food Loops.

        Operational, but not grim

        Push the visual design toward enterprise inventory software and it becomes dense and joyless, and nobody adopts it voluntarily. Push it toward a friendly consumer app and it stops looking like something you would trust with stock control.

        So the palette carries the warmth and the status system carries the urgency. They are deliberately separate: brand color never means anything operational, and status color is never decorative.

        What I would not claim

        We never tested this in a working kitchen during service, which is the only test that would really count. Our closest contact with the real context was a worker and a manager talking about their work, not doing it.

        The scanner also assumes barcodes, and a lot of what arrives at a restaurant is unlabeled: a crate of produce, a delivery from a local farm. That is the largest hole in the concept, and we did not solve it.

        And the system only reduces effort if scanning is faster than doing nothing. Doing nothing is very fast. That comparison is the one I would run first if this went further.
